Lead story · Product design · Yoto × Heirloom · Creative Bloq · 13 Nov

Designing for kids means knowing what to leave out

Yoto's new audio players for children were redesigned by product studio Heirloom. On Children's Day, their biggest lesson is a quiet one: the screen got better, but it was not allowed to become addictive.

A cult product, changed with care. Yoto is a card-based audio player for children that grew from a Kickstarter-style project into a well-loved brand with a vocal parent community. Studio Heirloom, whose team has worked on the player since its early versions, led the biggest change to its physical design yet. Yoto asked for more storage, a longer battery, a tougher inside and a splashproof body.

Shaped by small hands. Heirloom explored 10 to 15 concepts for one player. When children tried a rounder, pebble-like form, they held on to it naturally, so that became the recommendation. The dials were softened and made slightly longer so kids can grip them from any angle, and the smart button sits off-centre because children use the player from the front while holding it.

A screen with rules. The team worked to Yoto's own line, "kids in control and parents in charge". Both screens are sharper now, but the graphics stay in lo-fi pixel art. The designers dropped ideas such as a character that waves when the player sits idle, because it would pull attention. Success is measured by how often the player is used and whether it adds value to family life, not by screen time.

Less choice, better choice. Instead of digging through 200 cards, children now get 20 favourites plus daily radio and podcasts on the player itself. Parents can set a day list and a calmer night list in the app. New users should not have to learn anything, and old users should not find familiar controls doing something new. Hidden details, like tiny faces between the dials and a winking logo on the cable, are left to be discovered.

What this means for you

When you design for children, you also design for the parent's values. Write two or three rules before you sketch (for example, "no feature that asks for more screen time"), test the shape in real hands, and cut anything that grabs attention without adding value. The same test works for adult apps: measure whether people are helped, not how long they stay.

Prompt of the day · Test it, score it, then redesign it

The Swar Wall Chart

Happy Children's Day. Today's brief: a classroom wall chart of the 12 Hindi vowels for the invented kids' publisher Gilli Press, one simple icon per letter. Twelve Devanagari letters in the right order is one of the hardest tests for image AI.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: rebuild the chart in Figma with a real Devanagari font such as Mukta or Hind. Set the letters at 72 pt, print one tile and check it from 3 metres away, the distance from a child's desk to the classroom wall.

Version 1 · Any tool
A 3:4 classroom wall chart for children, flat screen-print style in three colours: warm yellow paper, deep blue and tomato red. At the top, the large Devanagari word "स्वर" and under it small text "Swar · Hindi Vowels". Below, exactly 12 rounded tiles in a 3-column, 4-row grid, in this order: अ with a pomegranate, आ with a mango, इ with a tamarind pod, ई with sugarcane, उ with an owl, ऊ with a ball of wool, ए with an ektara, ऐ with spectacles, ओ with a mortar and pestle, औ with a toolbox, अं with grapes, अः with a small star. Each tile: one big letter, one simple icon, no other words. Tiny "GILLI PRESS" at the bottom centre. No people, characters or brands.

Why it works: every letter is listed in order with its icon, so you can score the chart tile by tile.

Version 2 · Midjourney V8
flat screen-printed children's educational wall chart, 12 rounded tiles in a 3 by 4 grid, one simple flat icon per tile: pomegranate, mango, tamarind pod, sugarcane, owl, ball of wool, ektara, spectacles, mortar and pestle, toolbox, grapes, small star, warm yellow paper, deep blue and tomato red only, empty space in the top half of each tile and a wide empty band at the top of the page, risograph texture --ar 3:4 --style raw --s 100 --no text, letters, people, faces, characters, logos

V8 tip: make the icons only, then set the Devanagari yourself; AI still breaks matras and the anusvara dot.

What you are testing: whether step 2 keeps all 12 tiles and puts the right vowel above the right icon.

Graphic Design

Identity · Brand.ai × Mouthwash Studio · 13 Nov

One rule, many logos

For AI brand platform Brand.ai, Mouthwash Studio built its own tool that generates many versions of the symbol from one set of design rules. New forms go into a shared library and are used across touchpoints; because they follow the same rules, they still read as one family. On the website, the symbols move in response to nearby images and on menu hover.

Typeface · DDOTT × Morula · 13 Nov

A font that keeps the ink blots

Pen, by Dominique Thiemé and Valerio Monopoli, copies the tiny pools of ink a pen leaves when it pauses. Its skeleton comes from old technical drafting lettering, with neo-grotesque forms. Pen, Pen X and Pen XX (bigger blots) give 36 styles. A Twinline family draws each stroke as two lines, and an SVG version works with pen plotters and laser cutters.

Studio · Dum Dum Studio · 12 Nov

A studio that starts every job from zero

Monterrey's Dum Dum Studio, founded in 2015 by Mario Julián Gutiérrez and Sergio Martínez, mixes the playfulness of Dada and Fluxus with its city's industrial graphic history of steel, brewing and clean grids. Work ranges from a textured café identity to an animated mark for a cocktail bar. Their rule: balance function and feeling, planning and spontaneity.

UI / UX

NN/g · UX research · 13 Nov

Research at the speed of AI coding

With AI agents writing code, teams can ship, watch and change faster than they can run studies. NN/g's answer is "research-in-the-loop": researchers decide what each release must measure and help discover, turn that into tracking built into the product, and pair quick numbers with qualitative follow-ups. A bad release still reaches real people, so research changes shape, not importance.

WhatsApp · Android beta 2.26.46.2 · 14 Nov

Instants become Zaps

WhatsApp's new disappearing camera, opened from a lightning-bolt button in the Chats tab, is being renamed: photos and videos taken with it are now Zaps. They vanish after one view, are end-to-end encrypted and can go to anyone, including groups. Lesson: a short name that matches the icon and works as a verb ("send a Zap") is easier to learn.

Apple's square screen for the home

HomeView, Apple's $299 smart home hub, went on sale with a 7-inch square display on a tilting hinge. It recognises up to six household members by face to show their own content and controls, offers customisable faces, and has no App Store. Design psychology term of the day

Affordance

What an object's shape suggests you can do with it. A round, pebble-like player says "hold me"; a long, soft dial says "turn me". Good affordances need no instructions, which matters most for children and first-time users.

Try it: cover the labels on one screen of your project. Can a user still tell what can be tapped, dragged or turned? If not, fix the shape, depth or contrast before adding more text.
