Lead story · AI · Your portfolio · 30 Oct

One switch to tell AI: don't train on my work

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic say they will all honour a single "policy token" that website owners can add to their robots.txt file. For designers who show work on their own site, it could become the simplest way yet to say no to AI training.

What changes. Today, a site that wants to block AI training must name each company's crawler separately. The new shared token lets you set your AI training preference once, and every company that supports it should stop its crawlers from using your pages for training.

Where it comes from. The signal was proposed at the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) by Kevin Kelley, a standards lead at Anthropic. Future Tools reports the standard is called AIPREF and uses a shared user-agent named "__usage_ai-training".

When it works. Anthropic already follows it. Google and Microsoft plan support next year, and OpenAI says "coming months". Meta voiced support but did not say it will honour it. Microsoft adds separate controls for training and for "grounding" (AI answers that cite your page), and says opting out will not hide sites from Bing search.

The bigger fight. Other tools are already out there: the RSL standard lets publishers charge crawlers, and Cloudflare blocks AI bots by default. The same week, OpenAI shared court briefs from supporters arguing that AI training is fair use in its case with The New York Times.

What this means for you

If your portfolio lives on your own domain, watch for this token and add it to robots.txt once your host supports it. It only covers companies that choose to honour it, and it does nothing for work posted on Behance, Instagram or Dribbble, where the platform's settings decide. Keep full-resolution files off public pages and check each platform's AI settings today.

AI Desk

Canva · 29 Oct

Canva's Brush is a model just for editing designs

A week after its Craft image model, Canva launched Brush, an in-house vision-language model that reads both the content and the layout of a design. Ask it to restyle colours, change every header in a deck or turn a slide into an Instagram post. Canva says it is up to 69% faster and 46% cheaper per task than its earlier agent. It is in beta for Canva Pro; Brand Kit support is coming.

Apple · 27 Oct

Pixelmator Pro gets Generate Fill

A free Apple Creator Studio update in November lets you circle part of a photo in Pixelmator Pro, type a change and get a matching patch, upscaled so it is not soft. Final Cut Pro gets thousands of built-in MotionVFX templates and text-to-image. Student pricing is $2.99 a month.

Film · 23–27 Oct

China's first fully AI film flops in cinemas

"Sanxingdui: Future Past" opened on 10,000 screens but earned only about $180,000 against a $1.5 million budget. Yet Fortune notes over 95% of Chinese microdramas released early this year were AI-made. Short-form audiences accept AI; cinema audiences, so far, do not.

Retail · 30 Oct

Starbucks plans AI menus that change per customer

Starbucks' CEO said the chain will explore recognising customers as they arrive at the drive-thru and showing them personalised digital menus. Taco Bell and McDonald's are testing similar boards. Menu design is turning into a system of rules, not one fixed layout.

Prompt of the day · Test it, score it, then redesign it

The Unity Weave Poster

For National Unity Day, today's brief is a typographic exhibition poster for "EKSOOTRA", an invented handloom collective showing weavers from seven states together. Seven coloured threads must meet in one knot. AI loves to miscount threads, cut through letters and misspell Devanagari. Score your result before you use it.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: use the AI image only to study colour and texture. Rebuild the threads and knot as vector paths in Illustrator or Figma, set both scripts yourself, then make an A3 print version and a 4:5 Instagram version from the same grid.

Version 1 · Any tool
Portrait 2:3 typographic exhibition poster for "EKSOOTRA", an invented handloom collective. Off-white handmade paper background. Exactly 7 thick cotton threads in 7 colours (indigo, madder red, turmeric yellow, leaf green, saffron, black, rose pink) hang from the top edge and twist into one single knot near the centre. Below the knot, bold condensed sans-serif headline: "MANY THREADS. ONE CLOTH." Under it, in Devanagari: "एकसूत्र". At the bottom: "7–9 NOV · KALA GHAR, PUNE". No thread crosses any letter. Soft daylight, real fibre texture. No people, no logos, no other text.

Why it works: the count, the colours, the knot position and every word are fixed, so each of the five checks has one clear right answer.

Version 2 · Midjourney V8
minimal exhibition poster, off-white handmade paper, exactly seven thick cotton threads in indigo, madder red, turmeric yellow, leaf green, saffron, black and rose pink hanging from the top edge and twisting into one knot at the centre, large empty space below for type, soft daylight, macro fibre texture, Indian handloom, Swiss poster layout --ar 2:3 --style raw --stylize 200 --no text, letters, logo, people

V8 tip: ask for empty space, not words. Let Midjourney handle fibre and light, then set "MANY THREADS. ONE CLOTH." and "एकसूत्र" yourself.

Version 3 · Nano Banana
Step 1: Create a 2:3 portrait image on off-white handmade paper: exactly 7 thick cotton threads in 7 different colours hang from the top edge and twist into one knot near the centre. Keep the bottom 40% empty. No text.

Step 2 (same chat): Keep the threads and knot exactly the same. In the empty area add a bold condensed headline "MANY THREADS. ONE CLOTH.", then "एकसूत्र" in Devanagari, then "7–9 NOV · KALA GHAR, PUNE" at the bottom.

What you are testing: whether adding text in step 2 changes the thread count, moves the knot or breaks the Devanagari matras.

Version 4 · ChatGPT

Layout, top to bottom:

Graphic Design

Packaging · 29 Oct

A wine label that looks shot from a speeding train

Vinya Llarga means "long vineyard" in Catalan, so Barcelona studio Atipus wrapped the bottle in one long band of yellow, green and blue streaks, like a landscape blurred from a train window. On the back label the streaks come into focus as leaves. Everything else stays quiet: small condensed capitals, small text and a wax-sealed cap.

Report · 26 Oct

Stocksy says great images are about relationships, not subjects

Stocksy, an artist-owned co-op with 1,800 members and three million curated images, published "Visual Insights 2027: Existential Grammar". It argues stock search runs on nouns, but connection lives in five small words: by, for, with, between and against. In its words, an image that connects "is in conversation with its audience". Each word comes with questions you can use in a brief.

Campaign · 28–29 Oct

Apple lets Gen Z get messy with #ShotoniPhone

Apple's new Instagram post shows young creatives' iPhone photos: a Photo Booth-style mirror, a giant hand-made iPod nano and a fashion jacket made of tangled wired EarPods. Creative Bloq reads it as Apple moving away from slick minimalism towards nostalgia and playfulness.

UI / UX

Android · 30 Oct

Apple Music brings its glass look to Android

New on Android this week: version 7.0 swaps the red accent for blue, adds a floating, slightly see-through bottom bar and a separate mini-player, and goes edge-to-edge. Artist pages now take their background colour from the cover art. Unlike iOS, the bar does not shrink when you scroll, a reminder that one design system still bends to each platform.

Messaging · 30 Oct

WhatsApp makes busy groups easier to follow

A new "Mentions and replies" filter shows only unread groups where someone tagged or replied to you. Pinned messages can now stay pinned forever, people who join late can be sent recent messages with one tap on their name, and group status updates are seen only by that group and vanish after 24 hours.

Careers · 30 Oct

Is "experience design" the next name for UX?

Nielsen Norman Group sees more "experience designer" job titles. Reasons: roles are turning generalist, AI helps people work outside their speciality, and UX teams are tired of being seen as "just making things pretty". "UX" is still far more common, so read job descriptions, not titles.

Student Corner

Career advice · 26 Oct

Comfortable job, stale skills: stay or go?

In It's Nice That's Creative Career Conundrums column, a designer two years into a well-paid in-house job says their skills feel stale and they miss like-minded people. Panellist Katie Cadwell's answer starts from one idea: you deserve a chance to find a role that challenges you. Good reading if your first job has stopped teaching you.

Freelance · 29 Oct

How to leave a nightmare client politely

Creative Boom's Dear Boom asked creatives how to drop a client whose "simple" jobs never end. Check your contract's notice period, finish what you agreed, then write a short email: you will not take more work, here is the end date, and a sincere good wish. Do not add "maybe again sometime", or your boundary means nothing.

Design psychology term of the day

Default effect

Most people keep whatever option is already selected. That is why today's lead matters: whether AI training is "on" or "off" by default decides what happens to most people's work, because few ever change a setting.

Try it: audit one sign-up form you designed. List every pre-ticked box and pre-selected option, and ask: is this default best for the user, or only for the business?
