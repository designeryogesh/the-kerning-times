A metro station in Lyon gets 30,000 handmade tiles

Camille Walala's first permanent public artwork covers the platforms of a busy station with a tribute to a basketball legend and a landmark building.

What changed. The French-born, London-based artist is covering the platforms of Flachet–Alain Gilles metro station in Villeurbanne. The work is called Alley-Oop, and it uses more than 30,000 hand-made ceramic tiles.

Why it matters. The station serves around 5.7 million passengers a year. Most of them will meet the work on their daily commute, not in a gallery. Public work at this scale is a lesson in how design lives in a space people pass through quickly.

The style. Walala brings her Memphis-influenced style to the platforms. It is built from grids, triangles and stripes, the same shapes she used on Leyton High Street in East London.

The idea. The design pays tribute to a basketball legend and to an architectural landmark from the 1980s postmodern period. The subject is local, but the language is bold enough to read from the other end of the platform.

What this means for you

Walala's station shows that pattern can carry a place's identity. Before you start, ask how fast people will see your work. Test a public piece at walking speed, not only on a screen, and count how many shapes a commuter can read in three seconds.

The 2027 Wall Calendar

Today's brief: a 3:4 wall calendar cover for an invented studio, SAAL NAYA CO. The cover shows the year 2027 in big numerals, the Hindi line नया साल (new year) and a January grid with the month name जनवरी. It tests numerals, Devanagari spelling, the count of dates in the grid and a simple palette. No religious symbols, no deities, no people and no real brands.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: make the 2027 calendar cover for your family, your college club or a small shop near you. Put the year in the biggest type, keep one colour as the accent, and print the grid at 100% on A4 to check the spacing.

Version 1 · Any tool
A 3:4 vertical wall calendar cover on warm cream paper. At the top, the year 2027 in very large bold dark green numerals. Under it, the Hindi line नया साल in medium deep-green Devanagari. Lower down, a single January grid: the month name जनवरी above seven narrow columns of dates, 31 dates in total. The studio name SAAL NAYA CO. in small letters at the bottom. Flat screen-print look, three colours only: deep green, warm cream and one marigold accent. No people, no hands, no deities, no festival symbols, no logos.

Why it works: the year and the date count are easy to check by eye, and the Devanagari line tests spelling as well as layout.

V8 tip: Midjourney often redraws Devanagari. Set the Hindi lines as live type after generation, then count the dates by eye.

What you are testing: whether step 2 adds the text without moving the numerals or the grid.

Design lesson · for this week

Design the glance, not only the screen

A status that sits next to the camera is seen in a split second. Put only the one thing a user needs in that spot, and test it with a real phone held in one hand.

Student Corner

Term of the day

Anchoring effect

The first number or image you see becomes a reference point. Later choices move toward it, even when that first point was random. Price tags on packs use it all the time.

Student task

Take the grid to your own street

Take Walala's grid idea to a bus stop or a shop front in your city. Draw one 4×4 tile pattern on paper, then test it: can a friend read the stop name from ten steps away?
