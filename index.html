Lead story · Graphic Design · Identity · Turner Duckworth · 23 Nov

Maker's Mark dips its new identity in red wax

Five years after its last refresh, the Kentucky bourbon brand has a global identity built from its own farm and pressed into the same red wax that seals every bottle.

What changed. London agency Turner Duckworth built the system from stalks of soft red winter wheat, rings of American oak and plants native to Star Hill Farm, the distillery's home in Loretto, Kentucky. Each one was dipped and pressed into red wax.

Why it matters. The 2021 identity used a drawn version of the famous wax drip. This time the team used the real thing. The brand's story now sits in the material itself, not in an illustration of it.

The lettering. The typography is inspired by the handwriting of Margie Samuels, who founded Maker's Mark with her husband, Bill Samuels Sr, and who came up with the bottle, label and wax. The brand has always traded on her wit.

Process. Every bottle is still dipped in red wax by hand, a slow and messy process. The new identity follows the same logic, and it is easy to see why the agency chose it.

What this means for you

Look for the object your client already owns, then use it as the material of the identity, not just as a picture. Ask what the product does every day, and let that action shape the system. Test the idea on the packaging before you show it.

Prompt of the day · Guru Nanak Jayanti · Test it, score it, then redesign it

The Seva Kitchen Poster

Today's brief: a 2:3 poster for an invented community kitchen drive, SEVA PATH. The Hindi headline एक थाली, सबके लिए (One plate, for everyone) sits above five plates in a row. One spoon sits at the bottom with the line "Sun 6 Dec · 11 am". It tests exact text, the count of plates and an inclusive colour palette. No religious symbols, no deities, no faces and no logos of real places or brands.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: design a poster for a free meal, a clothes drive or a tutoring camp in your own area. Show five things people can do to help, in five simple shapes. Then ask a friend to tell you the date from the back of the room.

Version 1 · Any tool
A 2:3 vertical community poster on warm cream paper. Headline at the top in bold deep-green Devanagari: एक थाली, सबके लिए. Below it, five simple round plates in one row, each with a different simple shape of food. One plain spoon at the bottom centre. Under the spoon, the line "Sun 6 Dec · 11 am" in dark green sans-serif, and the small name SEVA PATH above the headline. Flat print look, three colours only: deep green, warm cream and one saffron accent. No people, no faces, no religious symbols, no logos.

Why it works: a fixed count of plates and one spoon are easy to check by eye, and the inclusive message tests tone as well as layout.

Version 2 · Midjourney V8
vertical community poster 2:3, warm cream paper, headline एक थाली, सबके लिए in bold deep-green Devanagari at top, five round plates in one row each with a simple food shape, one plain spoon bottom centre, small line Sun 6 Dec 11 am in dark green sans-serif, flat screen print, three colours only deep green cream saffron accent, no people, no religious symbols, no logos --ar 2:3 --style raw

V8 tip: Midjourney often redraws Devanagari. Set the headline as live type after generation, then count the plates by eye.

Version 3 · Nano Banana
Step 1: Create a 2:3 vertical poster on warm cream paper with exactly five round plates in one row, each with a different simple food shape, and one plain spoon at the bottom centre. No text yet. No people, no religious symbols.
Step 2 (same chat): Keep the plates, spoon and paper exactly the same. Add the headline एक थाली, सबके लिए in bold deep-green Devanagari at the top, the small name SEVA PATH above it, and the line Sun 6 Dec · 11 am under the spoon.

What you are testing: whether step 2 adds the text without moving the plates or the spoon.

Moving across cultures changes how you design

Design psychology · Term of the day

Serial position effect

In a list, people remember the first and last items best. The middle fades. Put your most important message at the start or the end, not inside a long menu.
