Lead story · UX careers · AI · 23 Oct

AI makes UX work faster, but designers are not happier

Nielsen Norman Group surveyed 521 UX practitioners about AI at work. Almost all of them have AI tools now, and most say tasks take less time. But the mood is only mildly positive, and many say the time saved is eaten up by new pressure and by fixing other people's AI output.

Who uses what. Over 99% had generative AI at work, but only 12% could pick any tool they liked; 59% were limited to a company-approved list. Two in three had used at least one agentic tool, from 85% at startups down to 36% at nonprofits and education bodies.

Where it helps. People rated AI's effect on task time at 4.2 out of 5 (3 means no change). The biggest savings were in coding, debugging and desk research. The smallest were in evaluating designs and testing with real users.

Where people hold back. 61% have never used AI to interview users, and 51% have never used it to test designs. Only 11% have tried AI interviewers, and 11% synthetic users. Output quality (54%) and data privacy (50%) were the top barriers.

How it feels. The average feeling was 3.57 out of 5. Of those who left comments, 69% said something negative. Many described mandates and usage tracking that push "performative" use. Designers rated their job security at 2.99 out of 5, below product managers at 3.81.

What this means for you

Speed is no longer your selling point; judgement is. Use AI for the slow, boring parts (research notes, first drafts, code), but keep real users, real testing and the final design call in your own hands. In interviews and reviews, show how you checked and corrected AI output, not just that you used it.

AI Desk

AI in products · 25 Oct

Just Eat pulls its AI food photos after menu mix-ups

Just Eat used AI to make dish pictures from menu text. "Tiger" prawns came out as tiger cubs and "toad in the hole" as a real toad. After users shared "AI vs reality" posts, the company apologised and began removing the images. Restaurants were judged on photos they never made.

Vector · 22 Oct

Ideogram Vector 1.0 makes editable SVG files

Ideogram's first vector model creates SVG artwork from a text prompt, or converts an existing image into vectors you can recolour and resize. It is in the Ideogram app and API, with image-to-vector starting at $0.10 per conversion. It reached Higgsfield on 23 October.

Gemini API · Shuts 29 Oct

Nano Banana 2 switches off tomorrow

New detail: Google will shut down Nano Banana 2 (gemini-3.1-flash-image) on 29 October, and developers are told to move to Nano Banana 2.1. If a plugin or app you use for client work runs on the old model, check it today.

Higgsfield's Content Factory turns a topic into a clip

Give it a topic and it produces a finished video from 15 seconds to 5 minutes, with 1 to 4 episodes per run and 50+ presets. Useful for drafts, but brand voice and fact-checking stay your job.

Prompt of the day · Test it, score it, then redesign it

The Diwali Sale Banner

Diwali is 11 days away and sale banners are everywhere. Today's brief is a performance ad: a 1.91:1 web and social banner for "VOLTKART", an invented Indian electronics store. Banners live or die on readable text, a clear button and safe margins, which are exactly where AI tools still slip.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: keep only the AI product shot and background. Rebuild all text, the button and the rangoli yourself in Figma or Illustrator, then make three sizes from it: 1200×628, 1080×1080 and 1080×1920. Note what you had to move in each size.

Version 1 · Any tool
Wide 1.91:1 Diwali sale web banner for "VOLTKART", an invented Indian electronics store. Left half: bold white headline "UP TO 40% OFF" and below it one rounded orange button that says "SHOP NOW". Right half: exactly 3 products on a deep plum background, wireless headphones, a smartwatch and a small speaker, lit by warm diya glow. Top-right corner: one symmetrical rangoli with exactly 8 petals. Keep all text inside a 10% safe margin. No people, no other text.

Why it works: the layout is split into two halves with fixed jobs, so the model does not scatter text over the products. Naming the margin forces breathing room.

Version 2 · Midjourney V8
e-commerce product banner, exactly three products on a deep plum backdrop: wireless headphones, smartwatch, compact speaker, warm diya light from below, soft gold bokeh, one symmetrical eight-petal rangoli in the top right corner, empty dark space on the left half for text, clean studio photography --ar 191:100 --v 8 --style raw --no people, text, letters

V8 tip: ask for empty space and add the headline and button later in Figma. Midjourney is great for the product plate, not for a sale headline you must spell right.

Version 3 · Nano Banana
Step 1: Create a wide 1.91:1 banner background, deep plum, with exactly 3 products on the right half: wireless headphones, a smartwatch and a small speaker, lit by warm diya glow. Add one symmetrical 8-petal rangoli in the top-right corner. Leave the left half empty.

Step 2 (same chat): Keep the products and rangoli exactly the same. On the left half add "UP TO 40% OFF" in bold white capitals and one rounded orange button below it reading "SHOP NOW". Keep all text 10% away from every edge.

What you are testing: whether adding text in step 2 moves or redraws the products. Count products and petals after each step.

Version 4 · ChatGPT
Create a wide 1.91:1 Diwali sale banner for "VOLTKART", an invented Indian electronics store.

Layout, left to right:
1. Left half: "UP TO 40% OFF" in bold white capitals
2. Under it: one rounded orange button, "SHOP NOW"
3. Right half: exactly 3 products (headphones, smartwatch, small speaker) on deep plum, warm diya glow
4. Top-right corner: one symmetrical rangoli, exactly 8 petals

Keep all text inside a 10% safe margin. No people, no logos, no other text.

Tip: if the button text is wrong, reply "fix only item 2, keep everything else". Then ask for a 1:1 version and check whether the layout still holds.

Branding · Opinion · 25 Oct

Why AI companies still hire humans for their brands

Creative Bloq notes a paradox: Ideogram was rebranded by How&How, Elva by Lazarev and Clusia by Together, and Meta's Muse logo was drawn by Jessica Hische. The writer's view is that brands are about trust, and AI firms need human craft to earn it. His advice: take the work and do it brilliantly.

Identity · Gaming · 23 Oct

EA FC's triangle learns to change shape

DixonBaxi has given the football game a new brand system for EAFC27. The 2023 triangle, which echoes the cursor above the player you control, is now a flexible shape that changes with context, built to move rather than sit still. Players are invited to make the brand their own.

Hospitality · Identity · 27 Oct

A bar brand that looks like a dream

London studio DutchScot branded Bar Rêve, a Brooklyn cocktail bar ("rêve" means dream in French). The script wordmark stretches and floats, the website asks you to float up instead of scrolling down, and a lamb, a swan and a bull hide in the drawings as small surprises.

Apple · Released 26 Oct

iOS 27.2 moves buttons to where thumbs are

In Apple Books, the Back button moves to the lower left and gets bigger, Close moves to the upper left, Bookmark becomes its own button, and progress bars show where you are. New in Music: the active tab turns blue instead of red, and widgets go black.

Patterns · 23 Oct

Which accordion icon works best? The caret

Smashing's Vitaly Friedman, drawing on NN/g testing, says a down-pointing caret is the safest accordion icon. Plus signs and arrows also work but mean too many other things. With no icon, people expect a new page. His other advice: avoid "split" menus where the label links away and the icon opens the list.

Design psychology term of the day

Automation bias

We tend to trust an answer more when a machine gives it, and we stop checking. That is how "tiger prawns" became tiger cubs on a live app, and why today's survey finds AI output creating cleanup work for others.

Try it: before you send any AI-made visual, check three things by hand: the spelling, the count of objects, and whether it matches the real product.
