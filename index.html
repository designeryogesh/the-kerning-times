Lead story · AI · Your daily workflow · 29 Oct

Figma is training its own AI to make your small edits

Figma has explained how it is building an AI model that understands its own file format. The goal is not flashy new screens but the small, everyday changes designers make all day: fix the spacing, change a colour, rework a layout after feedback.

Why its own model. Figma's engineering VP Josh Clemm writes that general AI models could not read Figma's file syntax, and teaching them with long instructions used too many tokens. So Figma post-trained smaller models on real design edits. An early model trained on more than 250,000 examples was already competitive with top models of that time.

How it learns now. Each training example pairs a starting design, an edit request and a correct result. Copying examples alone made the model less flexible, so Figma is adding reinforcement learning, with rewards based on criteria written by expert designers.

How it judges "good". Figma scores each edit on separate points instead of one vague quality mark. A design can have good alignment but poor contrast, or look polished and still miss the request. Common AI mistakes it checks for: icons overlapping text, clipped arrows and text sitting on text.

The results so far. Since the agent went from beta to all users this month, typical tasks are about 20% faster, user ratings are 33% higher and people undo its work 60% less often. Big third-party models still handle image generation, vectors and brand-new explorations; Figma's own model does the precise edits.

What this means for you

AI is coming for the repetitive edits first, not for your ideas. Two habits will pay off: write edit requests the way you would brief a junior ("move the tag to the top left, keep everything else"), and keep your files clean with real components and variables, because the agent reads your design system for context. Then check every AI edit for overlaps, clipping and contrast before you accept it.

AI Desk

Apple · 31 Oct

Apple's new CEO: no AI just for show

In an interview reported by TechCrunch, John Ternus, Apple's CEO since September, said Apple will "absolutely" use AI in new products, but "We don't want to ship AI for AI's sake." His test: people should not have to think about whether AI did it, only whether they love the result. A good rule for any AI feature you design.

Film · 31 Oct

A production designer's advice: use AI, don't fear it

Award-winning production designer Christopher Glass told Creative Bloq that AI is useful for rig removal, set extensions and quick reference images, but cannot replace human memories and feelings. His advice to young creatives: "Use their tools against them", stay curious and learn budgeting.

Sharper Seedance video on the Runway API

Seedance 2.0, Fast and Mini on the Runway API now take a "high" bitrate setting that keeps more detail and fewer compression artefacts, at the same price. Runway also published guides for Workflows: build a node pipeline in the app and run every step with one request.

Google · 28 Oct

Gemini 4 Carbon builds better web pages

Google's newest frontier model is mainly about coding and cyber security, but it also jumped 89 points to 4th place on WebDev Arena, a ranking for AI-built websites. It is going to selected security partners first, with wider access later.

Prompt of the day · Test it, score it, then redesign it

The Winter Chai Menu Board

November evenings are getting cooler, and chai stalls are getting busy. Today's brief is a chalkboard menu for "KADAK CORNER", an invented roadside chai stall. Menus are a hard test for AI: it skips items, changes prices, breaks the ₹ sign and garbles Devanagari. Score your result before you use it.

Score: 0 / 5

Student task: use the AI image only for mood and lighting. Rebuild the menu in Illustrator or Figma with a chalk-texture brush, set the prices on a proper grid, then make an A2 print version and a 9:16 Instagram story with one "today's special".

Graphic Design

Identity · 31 Oct

Sundance gets a logo that works like a film frame

Porto Rocha built the Sundance Film Festival identity around a black, round-cornered rectangle holding the name in white Monument Grotesk. It sits over film stills like a screen, and turns orange, green, pink or grey for venues, tickets and wristbands. The grid repeats the frame like film-strip cells. Our favourite detail: an embossed black-on-black version.

Rebrand · 28–31 Oct

A rebrand that began with a séance

To rebrand Purple Garden, an online psychic-services platform, Mother Design held a séance, a chakra reading and an automatic drawing workshop with charcoal. The shapes and textures from that session became the identity. Creative director Joanna Tulej says they "needed to allow some of that spontaneity into the way we designed it".

Packaging · 30 Oct

CVS shows why systems beat single packs

Pearlfisher's CVS store-brand redesign started with 68 pain-relief products and will cover nearly 3,000 by the end of 2026. "CVS Health" became just "CVS". Fixed rules: the mark on white at top, a flat colour block per category, a bold name, the count at the bottom and a "Compare to" tab. Pearlfisher's line: "Make the right choice obvious, then make it repeatable."

Design psychology term of the day

Law of common region

Things placed inside the same boundary feel like one group, even if they look different. It is why WhatsApp's new settings page wraps related options in rounded sections, and why Sundance's frame makes a ticket, a wristband and a poster feel like one family.

Try it: take one long settings screen or form you designed. Group the fields into 3 or 4 boxes with a light background, remove the divider lines, and ask a friend to find one option. Time both versions.
